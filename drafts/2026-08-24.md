## 1. Potency Assessment of Active Immunotherapy Products; Draft Guidance for Industry; Availability

**Why:** FDA guidance on potency assay development for immunotherapy products—directly relevant to IVD regulatory requirements.

**Summary:** FDA published a draft guidance on potency assessment for active immunotherapy products (ACTIMPs), covering assay design, validation, and evaluation. It supplements a December 2023 draft guidance on cellular and gene therapy potency assurance, and specifically addresses peptide- and protein-based ACTIMPs that fall outside the CGT category.

**Draft:**

FDA dropped a draft guidance on potency assessment for active immunotherapy products (ACTIMPs) this week. It's meant to supplement the December 2023 CGT potency assurance draft, but specifically covers peptide- and protein-based products that don't fall into the cellular or gene therapy bucket.

That carve-out is the part I keep thinking about. Potency is already one of the harder characterization problems in biologics, and getting clearer recommendations for this particular product class seems useful (if also, knowing guidance timelines, not exactly fast).

Still draft, so comments open before anything is finalized.

**Source:** Federal Register — https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/20/2026-16960/potency-assessment-of-active-immunotherapy-products-draft-guidance-for-industry-availability

## 2. It Takes More Than Insurance Coverage to Standardize NGS Testing in Practice, Study Shows

**Why:** NGS testing standardization study addresses clinical lab practices and test validation—relevant to CLIA/diagnostic regulation.

**Summary:** A study reported by GenomeWeb found that insurance coverage alone is not enough to standardize NGS testing in clinical practice. Other factors beyond coverage appear to drive variation in how and when NGS testing gets used.

**Draft:**

The headline from this GenomeWeb piece didn't surprise me, but it's still worth sitting with: insurance coverage alone doesn't standardize NGS testing in practice.

Working in diagnostics and clinical data, I've seen how coverage is treated like the finish line. Get the payer to say yes, and the access problem is solved. But there's a lot of space between 'covered' and 'consistently ordered, correctly interpreted, and actually used to change care.'

No idea yet what factors the study highlights specifically. Adding it to the reading pile. (The pile is, as always, ambitious.)

**Source:** GenomeWeb (POC) — https://www.genomeweb.com/cancer/it-takes-more-insurance-coverage-standardize-ngs-testing-practice-study-shows
